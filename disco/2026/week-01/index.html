The Lowdown

by the Commissioner

Your teams are like newborn babies at this point - full of potential, can do no wrong, and just require a bit of care and feeding to not completely die. They haven’t started school, haven’t gotten arrested or pregnant, and every single one of them could grow up to be a doctor, lawyer, astronaut, and/or multi-millionaire. Some of your teams (the one with Josh Jacobs, for example - EMCO’s) were born with birth defects, but she’s a Jets fan and used to suffering, and probably is still springing with hope eternal, not unlike our Bears fans. Anyway, best of luck everyone!

This league page was built using ChatGPT paired with Claude Code, which pushes to GitHub and then to Vercel to host the page. While some of the tabs are populated with AI-generated content, and I use AI to help research each section, the Lowdown and the matchups will always be typed up by yours-truly. A newsletter with no soul doesn’t rate a read as far as I’m concerned.

Come back with any BGCs!

Common Tactical Picture

Corn-Fed Fatties (Babe) vs Double TDs (Double)

Babe’s team is a 97th percentile baby; you know the one - nine months old, built like a bag of concrete, sleeps eleven hours a night and eats whatever hits the plastic portions plate. Three positional rooms, and only one of them is soft: 9th at TE, but 4/6th at WR/RB. On the other hand, Double TDs is a newborn. A newborn’s head runs about a quarter of its body length and the legs are strictly there for anatomical correctness, which is Double’s roster to a T - QB room 3rd, tight ends 4th, and a backfield ranked 12th of 12. Woof. Gorgeous baby dome but ornamental legs. The thing about newborns, though, is that they eventually learn to walk (and maybe run), and they generally get it done well before Week 14. Double just needs it to happen before kickoff.

Statistical Anomalies (McLovin) vs Secret Asian Man (POP)

POP built a perfectly average baby. All moving parts and internal organs are in place, but the normal reaction from most people will just be a polite “What a baby!” He’s 5th at RB, 7th at TE, and 11th at QB, thanks to Burrow, Bryce, and Penix. He dropped Tyreek Hill (his last pick) to claim AJ Barner, which is like putting underwear on an infant - mostly a waste of time. Ask him in December whether Bryce and Penix somehow found a way to make themselves relevant, and whether Burrow can remain healthy. We may not have kickers in this league, but I still found a way to punt with the TE position. Just like most babies, I’ll be streaming out of at least one orifice (the waiver wire) for the foreseeable future. However, my RBs and QBs (4/4 of my first picks) are great! My baby has the constitution of a teenager, but the WR/TE focus of a two year-old. My fix? Claiming Mike Gesicki off waivers, which is about as effective as yelling “Improve! Learn!” at a small child.

Tua Girls One Kupp (HOP) vs VHS Cowboys (Juan)

HOP and Juan brought similar babies to the party, just in different onesies. You know the types of parents - the ones who tell you their kid rolled over at three months, and then keep repeating it to everyone because it’s literally the only thing interesting about their baby. HOP’s trick is the WR room (2nd in the league, and genuinely wonderful with Drake London and Tet McMillan). Everything else is still gestating: 8th at RB, 9th at QB, 11th at TE. Juan’s “his first word was papa” is the QB room, and it is the best one in Disco, full stop (Josh Allen and his homey Dak). However, put that team down on the carpet and it just kinda flops around; you get 8th at TE and 11th at RB. His fix was five FAAB on Malik Davis, paid for by dropping Jerry Jeudy - steadying the high chair with a bit of bubblegum and duct tape. Both parents are going to spend Sunday saying “watch this, watch this!” and then just holding their one-tricks up. Whoever’s works best wins.

The Dude Abides (The Dude) vs Goff’n with the Boys (Sealed)

The Dude registered for everything and received three of each. Three tight ends, especially when one of them is Trey McBride, is like a crib for three different rooms - excessive. Dalton Kincaid and Pat Freiermuth are in the kitchen and basement. He still has 1st-ranked TE room I guess, and six running backs as well, headlined by Christian McCaffrey (Questionable, naturally) and Cam Skattebo. All of it was financed by the WR room, which is Amon-Ra St. Brown and then a closet of hand-me-downs, 11th in Disco. However, Sealed went the other direction entirely and assembled a receiving corps eligible for a group discount: Justin Jefferson, Tee Higgins, Chris Godwin and Keenan Allen in his thirteenth season. His tight end room is Hunter Henry, Dallas Goedert and Cade Otton, which are all onesies from the local thrift store. Sealed’s “he’s very advanced for his age” is De’Von Achane, young talent on an otherwise mature team. Sleeper says 54/46, 140.36 to 133.97, second-closest game on the board.

Love Sutton Brocks (EMCO) vs Hand me a White Claw (PITCH)

EMCO is the mom who gave two of her kids the same name: Love, Sutton, and two Brocks: Bowers and Purdy. The nursery is stocked to match: six running backs, led by Saquon Barkley and Josh Jacobs, good for the 2nd-ranked RB room, and Bowers anchoring the TE room tucks her in at 2nd there. The QB room lands 10th, which is what happens when your QB2 is Purdy (but not pretty) good. PITCH’s team is the third-born child: Nobody photographs anything anymore, the outlet covers are off, and sharp corners have become learning opportunities vs. pool noodle magnets. Her squad is a 19-pack of Sams Cola: 5th at WR, 5th at TE, 6th at QB, 7th at RB. Everything is fine and everything is middle-of-the-road. She’ll tell you the kid is happy and healthy and then change the subject to a more interesting topic like birdwatching or gardening. Sleeper says 57/43, 134.61 to 125.18.

Stafford and Sons (Fingers) vs Boutte QBs (DIP)

Both of these parents named the baby after its ugliest feature. Stafford and Sons is Matthew Stafford and Sam Darnold, a QB room that grades 12th of 12, which is what happens when you wait until the 7th round to draft a QB in SF. Fingers went ahead and adopted them anyway. He childproofed the nursery and the kitchen - (3 RBs and 3 WRs ion the first six rooms) but left the pool wide open (TE Kelce and Andrews). Instead of addressing positional need, he swapped Calvin Ridley for Zavion Thomas - two WRs. However, DIP went and had beaucoup (bonjour) QBs, four of them (Kyler, Cam Ward, Briskett and D-Watson) for two starting slots, one of whom who won’t start without some serious roster massaging. What actually keeps that baby alive are Ja’Marr Chase and Nico Collins, the 1st-ranked WR room in Disco, the first- and third-born children of his draft. And somehow he named his team after a 16th-round pick.

What’s in a Name? City Fantasy Team Build

The premise: build the best fantasy starting lineup in which every player’s own first or last name is exactly the name of a real city. One QB, two RB, two WR, one TE, one K. Exact matches only, so no nicknames and no partial credit for living inside a longer name.

The rule that decides everything is municipal class rather than size. The place has to be a city, which means a town, a village or a borough gets you nothing no matter how many people live there.

QB Josh Allen (BUF) — Allen, Texas · 104,627
RB Bijan Robinson (ATL) — Robinson, Illinois · 7,150
RB Jonathan Taylor (IND) — Taylor, Michigan · 63,409
WR Ja’Marr Chase (CIN) — Chase, Kansas · 396
WR Justin Jefferson (MIN) — Jefferson, Georgia · 13,233
TE Colston Loveland (CHI) — Loveland, Colorado · 76,378
K Brandon Aubrey (DAL) — Aubrey, Texas · 5,006

That is the consensus 2nd, 3rd, 8th, 11th and 27th players off the board, plus TE3 and the best kicker in football. I went looking for seven names that technically worked and came back with a first-round board that happens to carry a residency requirement. Chase, Kansas has 396 people in it and is carrying the WR1.

OUTSIDE CITY LIMITS

Three of the best players in football exist as municipalities, not cities:

Brock Bowers — TE1. Bowers, Delaware is a town of 278. Chase, Kansas is a city of 396.
Trey McBride — TE2. McBride, Michigan is a village of 189.

THE ALL-MARQUEE TEAM

Then somebody is going to point out that Chase, Kansas is not a proper city, so here is the same exercise with one extra rule: the city needs at least 100,000 residents.

This rule costs the roster the consensus RB1, RB2, WR1, TE3 and K1, and only one man survives from the first team.

QB Josh Allen (BUF) — Allen, Texas · 104,627
RB Omarion Hampton (LAC) — Hampton, Virginia · 137,148
RB Bucky Irving (TB) — Irving, Texas · 256,684
WR Drake London (ATL) — London, England · 9,089,736
WR Parker Washington (JAX) — Washington, D.C. · 689,545
TE Tyler Warren (IND) — Warren, Michigan · 139,387
K Tyler Loop (BAL) — Tyler, Texas · 105,995

London means the one in England, because London, Ohio is 10,279 people and fails the floor. Washington is here on a written exception: the Census Bureau’s own District of Columbia guide says the District has one city, its population glossary says the District is a county, and I picked the reading that got me a receiver.

And the kicker slot is the whole bit in one line. The entire qualifying pool in the National Football League is two men, and they are both Tylers (Loop and Bass).

Close, but no cigar: Lawrence, Kansas has 94,934 residents. Trevor Lawrence needed 5,066 more Jayhawks - not that he was going to beat Allen anyway.

Peer and Near-Peer Competition

There are better QB rooms, but nobody else combines this much high-end talent across RB, WR and TE.
  2. VHS Cowboys (Juan) · Peer Competition — Josh Allen plus Dak Prescott is the best starting QB combination in the league, which is an enormous head start in Superflex. A.J. Brown, Terry McLaurin and Marvin Harrison Jr. keep WR strong; the only reason this is not No. 1 is the thinner RB room.
  3.   4. Goff’n with the Boys (Sealed) · Near-Peer Competition — Justin Herbert and Jared Goff are a high-floor Superflex pair, and De’Von Achane, Justin Jefferson and Tee Higgins give the lineup genuine difference-makers. The roster falls off faster at RB/TE than the top three, but its starters are substantially better than an eighth-place automated ranking suggests.
  5. Corn-Fed Fatties (Babe) · Near-Peer Competition — Bo Nix and C.J. Stroud are a solid Superflex pairing, then Jonathan Taylor, Ashton Jeanty and Jaxon Smith-Njigba provide a strong skill-position core. Sam LaPorta is carrying a thin TE room, but there is enough balance here that very few weekly lineup spots should be outright liabilities.
  6. The Dude Abides (The Dude) · Near-Peer Competition — Christian McCaffrey, Amon-Ra St. Brown and Trey McBride give The Dude three elite anchors, with Trevor Lawrence and Daniel Jones forming a workable if unspectacular QB pair. WR depth behind Amon-Ra/Zay Flowers is the clearest reason this team sits outside the top five.
  7. Double TDs (Double) · Near-Peer Competition — Jalen Hurts and Jayden Daniels might be the nastiest QB pair in the league and can erase a lot of roster sins by themselves. The problem is that the sins are real: the site currently has the RB room 12th and WR room 10th, so the quarterbacks have to carry considerably more weight here than they do for the teams above.
  8. Statistical Anomalies (McLovin) · Competitive but Flawed — Lamar Jackson is exactly the kind of player you build a Superflex roster around, and the No. 1 RB room gives him real support. The current No. 12 TE room and middling WR construction leave too many points concentrated in Lamar and the backs, but the ceiling remains contender-level.
  9. Hand me a White Claw (PITCH) · Competitive but Flawed — Drake Maye and Baker Mayfield are a good Superflex pairing, while CeeDee Lamb, Chris Olave and Emeka Egbuka give the receiving corps enough ceiling. Almost every room lands around league-average, which makes this roster hard to exploit but also means there is no obvious second dominant position.
  10. Boutte QBs (DIP) · Competitive but Flawed — Ja’Marr Chase and Nico Collins form the league’s No. 1 WR room, but Kyler Murray/Cam Ward is a less secure Superflex pairing than the teams above and the RB room currently ranks 10th. This can absolutely work; it just needs the expensive WR advantage to overcome multiple merely adequate starting slots.
  11. Secret Asian Man (POP) · Competitive but Flawed — Joe Burrow gives POP a legitimate QB1 and James Cook/Kyren Williams provide useful RB strength, but Bryce Young/Michael Penix leave the second QB slot uncertain and no other room ranks as an obvious league advantage. Kyle Pitts’ Week 1 setup provides one potential path to outperform this ranking immediately.
  12. Stafford and Sons (Fingers) · Strategic Reassessment Required — Jahmyr Gibbs, Derrick Henry and Bucky Irving may be the best collection of running backs in the league, which makes last place look ridiculous until you remember this is Superflex. The site’s current evidence has Stafford 12th at QB and 12th at WR; Matthew Stafford/Sam Darnold cannot give away that much ground every week and expect the running backs to repair it.

Tracks of Interest

Kyle Pitts, TE (POP) — Atlanta at Pittsburgh

The Kyle Pitts experience has conditioned fantasy managers to approach good news the way an EOD tech approaches an unattended backpack, but this is one of the cleaner Week 1 setups on the board. Pitts finally delivered the season people had been drafting for years, finishing 2025 as the PPR TE2 with career highs in catches and touchdowns. FantasyPros has him around TE5 in PPR for Week 1, while RotoBaller specifically identifies Pittsburgh as a matchup to attack after the Steelers surrendered the second-most fantasy points to tight ends last season. Pittsburgh also finished 29th against the pass by yardage. This is less “maybe Pitts finally breaks out” and more “the breakout already happened, and Week 1 gives him a favorable runway to prove it was real.”

The play: Strong TE1. More interesting than the usual Pitts hype because both role and matchup now support it.

David Montgomery, RB (Juan) — Houston vs. Buffalo

Montgomery quietly has one of the better volume-plus-matchup profiles of the non-elite running backs this week. FantasyPros consensus has him around RB18, and RotoBaller expects him to lead Houston’s backfield with Woody Marks mixing in. The matchup is the appeal: Buffalo allowed the sixth-most fantasy points to running backs last season, while its run defense finished 28th in yardage allowed at 136.2 rushing yards per game. Montgomery does not need to monopolize touches to return RB2 value here; he mostly needs Houston to keep him in his normal early-down and scoring-area role.

The play: RB2 with a better ceiling than his relatively boring preseason profile suggests.

Bryce Young, QB (POP)— Carolina vs. Chicago

This is primarily a Superflex track, but a very useful one. RotoBaller calls Young a strong QB2 against a Chicago defense that allowed the fifth-most fantasy points to quarterbacks last season and is entering Week 1 with secondary injuries. FanDuel’s matchup data also notes that Chicago ranked only 22nd in passing defense at 227.2 yards allowed per game in 2025. Young does not need to suddenly become Josh Allen; against a depleted secondary, a clean 225–250 yards plus a couple of scoring opportunities is enough to make him a valuable QB2.

The play: Strong Superflex start and a good candidate to outperform more recognizable QB2 names.

Dallas Goedert, TE (Sealed)— Philadelphia vs. Washington

Goedert may be the least glamorous good decision of Week 1. FantasyPros has him roughly in the TE8–11 range depending on scoring, while RotoBaller notes he ranked top eight among tight ends last season in fantasy points per game, receiving-touchdown rate and target share. Washington allowed the fifth-most fantasy points to tight ends in 2025 and ranked 28th against the pass at 242.5 yards allowed per game. Add an Eagles offense that no longer has A.J. Brown absorbing targets, and Goedert has a perfectly plausible path to outperform his weekly rank.

The play: Low-end TE1 with legitimate top-six Week 1 upside.

MarShawn Lloyd, RB (DIP) — Green Bay at Minnesota

This one is here because the uncertainty itself is useful information. Josh Jacobs is unavailable, which has abruptly moved Lloyd toward the front of Green Bay’s backfield. RotoBaller expects Lloyd to handle the bulk of the carries and calls him a reliable RB2; FantasyPros currently projects roughly 13 carries, 52 rushing yards and 9.6 standard points. Other consensus sources are far more skeptical—one aggregation has him around RB48–49—which tells you the market has not agreed on how much to trust a player with almost no meaningful NFL workload. That disagreement creates the opportunity. If Green Bay actually gives him 15+ touches, the pessimistic rankings will look stale almost immediately.

The play: Volatile RB2/FLEX rather than a safe start. Of everyone on this list, he is the player I most want to watch because Week 1 could radically reset his value.

Fades

Jordan Love, QB (EMCO) — Green Bay at Minnesota

The name is considerably better than the matchup. Minnesota allowed the fewest fantasy points to quarterbacks last season, and Love managed just 139 passing yards and 7.1 fantasy points in his 2025 meeting with the Vikings. FanDuel also grades Minnesota as last year’s No. 2 pass defense, allowing only 158.5 passing yards per game, and projects Love for a modest 15.5 fantasy points. In a 1QB league, there are enough alternatives that this is a matchup I would rather avoid.

The fade: Sit in normal 1QB formats if you have a credible alternative. Still usable in Superflex.

Rashee Rice, WR (EMCO) — Kansas City vs. Denver

Rice’s role makes him difficult to outright bench, but this is a bad week to expect WR1 production. Denver ranked seventh in pass defense last season, and Rice scored only 9.8 PPR points against the Broncos in their previous meeting. Patrick Surtain II is part of the problem, and RotoBaller drops Rice into low-end WR2 territory for Week 1. FanDuel’s current projection is even colder at 9.2 fantasy points. The volume can rescue him; the matchup makes the ceiling much less attractive.

The fade: Start if roster construction forces it, but treat him more like a WR2/FLEX than the player his name suggests.

Terry McLaurin, WR (Juan) — Washington at Philadelphia

McLaurin is a particularly good example of why a weekly section should differ from season-long rankings. FantasyPros still has him around WR22 in PPR, but the matchup is nasty: RotoBaller expects him to see Quinyon Mitchell, who ranked first among corners in fantasy points allowed per target last season. Philadelphia finished eighth against the pass, and FanDuel currently projects McLaurin for just 8.2 PPR points, 49 receiving yards and 0.24 TDs. There is touchdown upside, but the median outcome looks much more FLEX-like than WR2-like.

The fade: FLEX rather than comfortable WR2. I would start a strong alternative over him.

Jeremiyah Love, RB (POP) — Arizona at L.A. Chargers

This is the easiest health-related fade. Love is recovering from a high-ankle sprain and was described this week as roughly 50/50 to play. Even if active, DraftSharks expects a potentially reduced workload, while RotoBaller notes that the Chargers allowed the fourth-fewest fantasy points to running backs last season. FantasyPros still projects usable production if he plays—roughly 12 carries, 54 rushing yards and three catches—but that assumes enough workload actually materializes. A rookie coming off a high-ankle sprain in his NFL debut against a strong run defense is exactly the sort of risk fantasy managers talk themselves into because of draft capital.

The fade: Bench unless practice reports materially improve and you lack another viable RB2/FLEX.

DK Metcalf, WR (Double) — Pittsburgh vs. Atlanta

Metcalf is expected to play, but there are two separate reasons for caution. First, he has been nursing an injury entering Week 1. Second, RotoBaller expects a possible shadow assignment from A.J. Terrell Jr. and ranks Metcalf as a lower-end WR3 for this matchup. Broader consensus is somewhat more optimistic—FantasyPros describes him as a strong FLEX, while another consensus aggregation places him around WR32—so this is not a universal sit. That disagreement is precisely why he belongs here: the downside case is stronger than his recognizable name suggests.

The fade: FLEX-level play. I would not treat him as an automatic WR2 in Week 1.
